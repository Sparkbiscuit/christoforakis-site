Two apps for problems close to home

Nicholas Christoforakis1 1Neuroscience, Middlebury College, Middlebury, Vermont. Correspondence: nick@christoforakis.com

I study neuroscience at Middlebury and build iOS apps for problems close to home. Filuma turns a task, a deadline and a rough estimate of effort into blocks of work around the rest of a day, with ADHD planning challenges in mind. Meds Ahead began with my family’s need to coordinate medications and refills, and it shows which medication runs out next, and why. Both are written in SwiftUI and SwiftData, and both are on the App Store. The figures that follow come from the apps, and two of them respond to input.

Both apps started with a problem in my own week. For Filuma it was making time for the work in front of me: knowing what to do was rarely the hard part, and starting it often was. A plan, in other words, has to survive the day.

For Meds Ahead it was a question my family kept asking. Knowing what was due was only part of it; we also needed to know what was running low, and a list of reminders could not tell us that.

Section 1 describes Filuma and Section 2 describes Meds Ahead. Section 3 is about Aloft, a flight game I build for fun, which supplied the plane on the home page.

Keywords: scheduling, ADHD, medication supply, SwiftUI, a cappella (see Acknowledgements)

Two apps for problems close to home

1Filuma

Filuma places blocks of work around existing commitments and gives earlier deadlines priority when it replans. If the remaining work cannot fit, it says so, so that the deadline or the estimate can be reconsidered instead of quietly missed. Apple Calendar can supply busy time, and Filuma can write its blocks to a calendar of their own; each direction is optional.

A day rarely goes to plan, so the plan has to move with it. Fig. 2 shows three versions of one afternoon: on time, half an hour late, and cut short. Time spent and work finished are kept apart, and unfinished effort stays in the plan instead of disappearing.

Starting is the one step a plan cannot take for you, so Filuma tries to make it smaller. The optional first step on the Focus card (Fig. 1b) also appears in the session timer and in the notification when a block begins, so there is nothing to reconstruct before you begin.

Filuma is on the App Store. The best test is one task with a real deadline: does the plan make sense, and is the next step clear? If something gets in the way, I would like to hear about it.

2Meds Ahead

Supply in Meds Ahead comes from recorded changes: refills, corrections and the doses you log. Each count keeps that history, so an estimate always has something concrete behind it, and every run-out date has a Why this date? view that shows its working. Labels and supported barcodes are read on the phone, and you confirm what the camera finds before anything is saved.

Meds Ahead is an organization tool, not medical advice. Follow your prescription label and your clinician’s instructions.

A forecast is only useful if you can see why it says what it says. In Fig. 4, one medication starts with 34 tablets on October 9 and one scheduled dose a day. Record a refill, or skip a dose, and the date moves; the table beside the chart keeps the arithmetic in view.

Data availability. Nothing leaves your iPhone. Records stay on the device, there is no account to create, and there are no ads or third-party analytics.

3Aloft

Aloft is a flight game that runs in a browser: island hopping in airliners, from a mainland hub out to the kinds of runways pilots tell stories about. Table 1 lists six of them. It does not solve a problem close to home, unless a short runway counts.

Methods. Both apps are written in SwiftUI and SwiftData; Filuma also uses EventKit and Meds Ahead uses Vision. I decide what each app should do. Claude and I plan, write and test the work together, and every change goes through testing and revision before it ships. I am learning to keep that process focused: fewer active projects, clearer responsibilities, and less time coordinating work that does not help solve the problem in front of me.
